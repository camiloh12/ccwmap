# Federal carry law for the national federal wave (research memo, 2026-10-25)

This is desk research by Claude standing in for the lawyer consult the
pre-populate spec recommended before the first national wave. It is not legal
advice. Each answer carries a confidence level: how likely the statement is
to be right as of 2026-10-25. The law moved a lot in 2026, so the
"last verified" dates matter.

## Summary

The federal-building ban (18 USC §930(a)) is still on the books, but on
2026-08-12 the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel read its
"other lawful purposes" exception (§930(d)(3)) to allow lawful self-defense
carry in federal facilities "generally open to the public". DOJ repeated that
position in court on 2026-10-02 for National Park visitor centers and shops.
The opinion binds the Executive Branch (so no federal prosecution while it
stands), can be withdrawn by a later Attorney General, and conflicts with
three pre-*Bruen* district court rulings. No appellate court has ruled.

So "federal building = no carry, high confidence" is no longer true for
ordinary public-facing federal offices. It remains true for federal
courthouses, VA property, prisons and active military installations, each
of which has its own rule that the opinion does not touch.

## Questions and answers

### 1. Is carry banned in GSA-listed federal buildings nationwide?

**Statute:** §930(a) makes knowing possession of a firearm in a "Federal
facility" a crime (up to 1 year). §930(g)(1): a Federal facility is "a
building or part thereof owned or leased by the Federal Government, where
Federal employees are regularly present for the purpose of performing their
official duties." §930(d)(3) exempts "the lawful carrying of firearms …
incident to hunting or other lawful purposes." §930(h): notice must be
posted at each public entrance, and absent posting there is no conviction
unless the person had actual notice. GSA's own rule, 41 CFR 102-74.440, adds
nothing: it restates §930.

**2026 shift:** *Application of 18 U.S.C. § 930(a) to Post Offices*, 50 Op.
O.L.C. __ (Aug. 12, 2026): "carrying arms for self-defense when entering a
building held open to the public is a paradigmatic 'lawful purpose'", so
§930(d)(3) covers it. The opinion disagrees with *De la Cruz-Bancroft*
(D.N.M. 2010), *Yorzinski* and *Tagore* (S.D. Tex. 2011), which required the
lawful purpose to relate to the facility. Footnote 12 limits the
*constitutional* analysis to post offices, but the *statutory* reading of
(d)(3) is general, and DOJ applied it to NPS buildings in *Zimmerman v.
Blanche*, No. 4:26-cv-372-Y (N.D. Tex.), Dkt. 22 (Oct. 2, 2026): §930(a)
"permits the lawful carrying of constitutionally protected firearms for
self-defense in federal facilities generally open to the public."

**What has not changed:** the statute; posted signs at federal entrances;
Federal Protective Service screening at most offices (the opinion itself
treats access-controlled places such as bases and airport sterile areas as
"something less than the general public"); the NPS website, which still
says firearms are prohibited in its facilities.

**Answer:** on the books, yes. In practice, as of today, a person who may
lawfully carry under state law and walks into a public-lobby federal office
is, per DOJ, not committing a federal crime; they will still be stopped at
any screening point and will still see a §930(h) sign.

- Confidence that the ban is the written law and could be enforced again
  after a change of administration or an adverse court ruling: **high (90%)**.
- Confidence that DOJ would not prosecute lawful self-defense carry in a
  public-facing federal office today: **medium-high (75%)**.
- Confidence that a screened federal building (magnetometer at the door)
  still functions as a no-carry zone: **high (85%)**.

### 2. Which FRPP buildings count as "Federal facilities"?

Only buildings where federal employees are "regularly present … performing
their official duties." No court has defined "regularly present"; the plain
text excludes buildings nobody staffs. Family housing (employees live there,
they do not work there), restrooms, barracks, radio and communications
sites, navigation aids and unstaffed storage do not qualify. Offices,
courthouses, laboratories, hospitals and clinics, prisons, ports of entry
and border stations, public-facing facilities, schools, museums, child care
centers and data centers do.

**Answer:** the design's use-based filter is legally right, not just
cautious: a red pin on a trailhead restroom asserts a prohibition that does
not exist. **Confidence: high (85%).**

### 3. Do grounds and parking lots count?

§930 covers "a building or part thereof." Grounds are not covered. Separate
regulations cover grounds for specific agencies: VA (38 CFR 1.218, "all
property under the charge and control of VA"), the Postal Service (39 CFR
232.1(l), "postal property"), Army Corps recreation projects (36 CFR
327.13). GSA property has no such rule.

**Answer:** for GSA buildings the zone is the building. The app's point pin
does not assert a radius, so no change needed. **Confidence: high (85%).**

### 4. Leased space in a multi-tenant commercial building

§930(g)(1) says "building or part thereof … leased by the Federal
Government." Only the leased part is the facility. FRPP has 12,565 leased
buildings; many are floors in private office towers. A pin on the building
implies the lobby, the coffee shop and the other tenants are no-carry, which
is wrong. No case law on common areas was found.

**Answer:** leased rows are legally correct pins with a misleading footprint.
Mark them lower-confidence with a caveat ("federal offices inside; applies
to the leased space"), or label them by agency and floor where FRPP gives an
address. **Confidence in the legal reading: high (85%).**

### 5. Federal courthouses

§930(e) bans firearms in a "Federal court facility" (courtrooms, chambers,
clerk, U.S. Attorney, Marshal and probation offices, adjoining corridors) with
up to 2 years. §930(e)(2) applies only the (d)(1) and (d)(2) exceptions:
the "lawful purposes" exception does **not** reach courts. The OLC opinion
says so expressly (p. 26). The rest of the courthouse is a Federal facility
under §930(a), and the U.S. Marshals screen every public entrance. Local
standing orders (e.g. W.D. Va., Aug. 2025) restate the ban.

**Answer:** no-carry, no exception for permit holders, unaffected by 2026
developments. **Confidence: very high (95%).** Keep `high`.

### 6. VA medical centers and clinics

38 CFR 1.218(a)(13): "No person while on property shall carry firearms,
other dangerous or deadly weapons, or explosives, either openly or
concealed, except for official purposes." Covers all VA-controlled property
including grounds, driveways and parking. Fine $500 (1.218(b)(37)). The OLC
opinion struck the Postal Service's parallel rule *as applied to public post
offices* because it has no (d)(3)-type exception; the same logic could reach
VA, but OLC confined itself to post offices, VA has not changed its rule,
and no VA litigation was found.

**Answer:** no-carry on VA property today, with a live argument against it.
**Confidence: high (85%).** Keep `high`, revisit if a VA case or a new OLC
opinion appears.

### 7. Military installations

Civilians cannot carry on active installations. Installation regulations
(Fort Bragg 190-11-1, Fort Riley 190-1, Fort Leonard Wood 190-6, MCoE
190-11) say state permits "are not recognized or valid" on post; visitors
must register firearms at the gate; 18 USC 1382 covers unlawful entry.
Secretary Hegseth's memo of 2026-04-02 presumes approval for **uniformed
service members** to carry privately owned firearms on base, not for
visitors, dependents or contractors. The OLC opinion lists installations as
access-controlled places outside its reasoning.

**But** 262 of MIRTA's 825 boundaries (32%) are National Guard sites (192
Army Guard, 70 Air Guard). Guard armories and bases are state-controlled
under Title 32, and carry there follows state law and the Adjutant General's
policy: South Carolina bars it, Kentucky allows members with permits,
North Carolina designates members, Michigan enacted a 2018 bill letting
permit holders carry at armories (final text not verified). Federal §930(a)
still applies to any Guard building where federal employees (Title 5
technicians, AGRs) regularly work, now subject to the (d)(3) reading.

**Answer:**
- Active federal installations (usa, usn, usaf, usmc, reserves, WHS):
  no-carry for civilians. **Confidence: very high (95%).** Keep `high`.
- National Guard sites: varies by state. **Confidence that a given armory is
  no-carry for a civilian: medium (60%).** Use `medium` confidence, or a
  per-state cell later.

### 8. National Park, Forest Service and Fish & Wildlife buildings

Park grounds follow state law (54 USC 104906). Buildings fall under
§930(a), and DOJ now says visitor centers and shops open to the public are
within (d)(3). NPS signage and its website still say prohibited. The
design's filter already drops housing, restrooms and most unstaffed park
buildings; visitor centers survive only through the "Other Institutional
Uses" bucket when FRPP reports employees.

**Answer:** unsettled. **Confidence that a park visitor center is a no-carry
zone in practice: medium (50%).** `medium` confidence at most; a case for
leaving Interior and Forest Service public-facing buildings out until
*Zimmerman* is decided.

### 9. Commercial airports

49 CFR 1540.111 and 49 USC 46505 cover the sterile area and aircraft; the
OLC opinion treats them as a separate regime. The non-sterile terminal is
state law (California bans carry anywhere under airport control; most states
allow it). The existing pin asserts the secured area only.

**Answer:** unchanged. **Confidence: very high (95%).** Keep `high`.

### 10. Bureau of Indian Education schools (668 BIA "School" rows)

Federal employees work there, so they are Federal facilities. Schools are a
*Heller* "sensitive place", a BIE school is not "open to the public" in the
post-office sense, and tribal law usually bans weapons on school grounds.

**Answer:** no-carry. **Confidence: high (80%).** Keep `high`.

### 11. Post offices (deferred source)

After the OLC opinion, DOJ dropped its appeal in *FPC v. Blanche* (N.D. Tex.,
injunction for FPC/SAF members, 2025-09-30), *Nastri v. Bondi* (D. Conn.,
March 2026) upheld the ban, and *Hornbake v. Blanche* (W.D. Pa., filed
2026-05-26) is pending. DOJ says it will not enforce §930(a) or 39 CFR
232.1(l) against lawful self-defense carry in public post offices.

**Answer:** when post offices are added, they are **UNCERTAIN (yellow)**,
not red, until a court or Congress settles it. **Confidence: medium-high
(70%).**

### 12. The app's exposure for a wrong pin

No case law on gun-free-zone maps was found. General negligent-
misrepresentation risk is low where the app shows citations, the
"verify locally" caveat and terms disclaiming legal advice. The dangerous
error is a false **green** (telling someone carry is allowed where it is
not); a false red costs a user convenience, not liberty. Under the 2026
reading, keeping federal offices red is the conservative error.

**Answer:** keep erring red; keep the caveat and citations visible; have the
terms of service reviewed once by a lawyer. **Confidence: medium (60%).**
This is the one question that still deserves a real lawyer.

## What this means for the Phase 8a design

1. Keep the use-based filter (Q2). It matches the statute, not just caution.
2. Split the single `US / FEDERAL_PROPERTY` cell's confidence by what the
   building is:
   - `high` (status locked, migration 013): courthouses, VA hospitals and
     clinics, prisons and detention, ports of entry and border stations,
     BIE schools, active military installations.
   - `medium` (editable, orange "verify locally" caveat): ordinary offices,
     laboratories, public-facing facilities, leased space, National Guard
     sites, Interior and Forest Service public buildings.
3. Update the cell's `citation` and `notes` to name §930(d)(3) and the
   2026-08-12 OLC opinion, and set `last_verified_date: 2026-10-25`.
4. Watch list: *Zimmerman v. Blanche* (N.D. Tex. 4:26-cv-372-Y), *Hornbake
   v. Blanche* (W.D. Pa.), any Fifth Circuit ruling on §930(a), any
   withdrawal of the OLC opinion, any VA change to 38 CFR 1.218.
